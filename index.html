The category

What we actually sell

AI as a service is not a bundle of automations, and it is not AI added to a CRM. It is the work of translating a company's real operating context into repeatable, governed capacity that lives inside the systems they already run.

That translation is the product. A client's judgment currently lives in experienced operators, chat threads, manager preferences and institutional memory — which works while humans do the work by hand, and fails the moment the business wants a system to act. Our job is to make that judgment explicit enough for a system to carry: expressed through workflows, knowledge structures, permissions, escalation rules, reporting logic and human handoffs.

Bespoke where the business is bespoke. Standardized where the operating pattern repeats.
Most of the market flattens one way or the other
  • AI as a generic productivity layer — easy to buy, hard to operationalize. Nothing in the client's actual operating context gets touched, so nothing durable changes.
  • Every use case as bespoke invention — impressive, and impossible to scale. Each engagement starts from zero and the practice never compounds.
Why the category is more useful than either

Because the interesting question is never "can AI do this" — it is which parts of this business are genuinely particular, and which parts only look particular. That is an operating judgment, and it is the thing we are actually paid for.

Practically, we help a client define four things
  • What work can be delegated to a system.
  • What judgment must stay human — and why that boundary sits where it does.
  • What context has to be made durable so the system can act without a person re-supplying it.
  • How the business gets visibility while the work happens, rather than after someone reconstructs it for a report.
Read those four again

They are the spine of every scoping conversation you will have. If a proposal does not answer all four, it is not finished.

The layer behind the skills

Access, scope, and the portal you're actually in

Skills behave the way they do because of what sits underneath them. You don't need to build an MCP server to work here, but you do need to know what authorizes a session, what bounds it, and what happens when the boundary is hit — because every one of those shows up as behavior you'll have to interpret in front of a client.

01
You bring the token

A client-specific Private App token, created in the client's own HubSpot, provided at the start of the session. No token means no client work — not degraded client work.

02
The server validates and names the portal

It checks the token against a low-cost endpoint and confirms the portal name back to you. Read that confirmation. It is your only cheap proof you're in the right environment.

03
Scopes set the ceiling

What the token is scoped for is what the session can reach. Capability is configurable per client, and it lives in HubSpot — not in the connector.

04
Missing scope surfaces cleanly

A 403 comes back as a normalized scope-missing signal rather than a silent failure. Add the scope to the Private App and the capability expands immediately — no connector change.

05
Trust decays with session age

Roughly: first 15 minutes proceed freely, 15–30 a soft checkpoint before any write, past 30 re-confirm token and portal. Long sessions drift; the tiers exist because of it.

Before meaningful client work — answer all five
  • What client portal am I in? Named, confirmed, not assumed.
  • What skill owns this lane? And is it the cheapest one that does?
  • Is this read-only or write-capable?
  • Does this require approval before execution?
  • What output am I expecting? If you can't state it, you're not ready to start.
Also true and worth knowing

Several skills log their own run to the Notion actions log as background telemetry. It's silent by design and doesn't affect session flow — but it means skill usage is observable, and that's a feature: it's how we learn which lanes actually get used.

Never assume
  • That the portal is right because the last session was. Session context drifts, especially across a day of client work.
  • That a name describes behavior. A workflow called "Trigger Forth Update" may push nothing anywhere. Open the definition and read the actions.
  • That an existing asset is a working asset. A property group at 0% fill and a disabled workflow are both real work and both deliver nothing.
  • That an integration runs because someone said it exists. An unverified hop gets reported as unverified or not at all.
  • That a scope error is a tool bug. It's usually the token telling you the truth about your access.
  • That silence means success. Confirm writes landed; don't infer it.

What the lab cannot teach you

The demo portal has no engagement record behind it — no scope document, no dated commitments, no meeting history. Repeatable method

Playbooks

Four jobs, in the order you'll actually hit them: get up to speed on an account, prepare for the meeting, log what came out of it, and scope anything new through the Cartographer. Almost everything you do in your first month is one of these four. The method is here; the facts are in the authenticated systems.

One thing to understand before you start. The accounts you're inheriting mostly predate the Cartographer — they were scoped before it existed, so there's no audit, no scope ledger, and no structured recommendation sitting behind them. That's why jobs 1 to 3 carry the weight on transferred work: you reconstruct the scope that was never formally mapped, and you keep meetings and follow-up tight enough that the gap doesn't compound. Job 1 — every account you inherit, before you touch anything

Get up to speed on an account

An inventory tells you what exists. It does not tell you whether the build is coherent, whether it's finished, or whether the client will get what they asked for. Those questions need intent, and intent is not stored in the CRM — it's in the dated engagement record. Join the two on time and attribution and an orphan field list becomes a traceable response to a stated requirement, built by a specific person, in a specific week. That join is the whole method.

Three ways this goes wrong — all three are live risks

Fabricated gaps. You propose a normative design, notice your own proposal has unspecified values, then report those as things the client failed to provide. If a requirement can't be traced to a client statement or an existing asset, it's a Zipcio recommendation — label it as one.

Inference dressed as fact. Reading a workflow's name and reporting its behavior. Names lie.

Confident arrows. Drawing a data-flow connector because someone said the integration exists. A swimlane full of clean arrows that don't move data is worse than a sparse one that's true.

Retrieval discipline — breadth first, then targeted depth

A months-long engagement plus a full portal read will exhaust any context budget if you pull both at full depth. Stage it, and write everything durable to a working file as you go so you never re-pull a source you've already read.

W1
Breadth — cheap and wide

The contracted scope document from Drive (required, read in full — it's the reference every later "was this in scope" question resolves against). Then the meeting list with dates, participants, and whether a transcript exists. Then meeting summaries only. Then the ClickUp space including closed tasks. Then a Drive file listing. Then Notion for canonical method and any prior documentation.

W2
Depth — only where flagged

Pull full transcripts and threads only for calls whose summary shows kickoff or requirements gathering, first mention of a system, decision language ("we'll go with", "instead of", "the reason is"), scope or timeline change, a new named stakeholder, or a reversal. Recency is not a selection criterion — the calls that explain a build are usually the early ones.

W3
Gap-directed

When the portal read surfaces a cluster nothing explains, go back for a targeted read against that specific date window. What isn't permitted is re-reading the record hunting for something that would justify the cluster.

Extract the reasoning, not just the ask

"They want a licensed-state filter" is half a finding. "They want a licensed-state filter because they were fined for contacting a non-licensed state and the compliance officer now signs off on every list" is the whole one — and it's the half that makes every downstream build decision legible.

The eight questions — answer in order, in writing

Your notes below persist in this browser only. Use them as a scratchpad, then move the real output into the engagement's own records.

Scoring what you found — existence is not function

Most overstated build assessments come from counting assets rather than working paths. Score the weakest link, not the average: a workstream needing five components to function sits at the level of its lowest one. Three components live and one at zero is a workstream at zero, because the outcome doesn't happen. Report the component detail alongside so distance-to-done stays visible — but never average a broken path into looking partly operational, and never lead with a single overall percentage.

0
Not started

No assets exist. Nothing built toward this outcome.

1
Scaffolded

Assets exist and are explicitly incomplete — placeholder workflows, routing with no pool, objects with no properties. Real intent, no functional path.

2
Built dark

Substantively complete, nothing flowing. 0% fill. Finished-and-disabled. Designed, never connected. The most commonly misreported state, in both directions.

3
Live partial

Data flows through some of the path. Verify with fill rates and record recency — presence of data is not proof of health.

4
Live verified

Full path functions and has been confirmed with evidence. Verified means observed, not assumed.

Anchor every score to the thing that produced it — the fill rate, the enabled state, the record recency, the missing component. A score without an anchor is an opinion.

Grade every link you draw
  • Attributed — a client statement, email, or task explicitly names this. Cite the source and date.
  • Inferred — timing, authorship, and subject align closely but nothing states the connection. Say so, and name the event you inferred from.
  • Unattributed — the asset exists and nothing explains it. That is a legitimate finding. Report it as unattributed rather than inventing a rationale.

Run the join in both directions and treat them as separate passes. Portal → requirement finds orphans. Requirement → portal finds what the build never served — and those are invisible to the forward pass. Never upgrade a grade to make a narrative cleaner.

Two habits that keep you credible
  • Report corrections loudly. When what you find contradicts something established earlier in the engagement — including something Zipcio itself asserted — that contradiction is usually the most valuable output of the exercise. Surface it early and plainly.
  • Distinguish Zipcio-owed from client-owed relentlessly. An assessment that lists the client's failures while omitting our own incomplete wiring will be read as defensive, and it will be right to be read that way.
  • Prefer stopping to guessing. Running out of evidence is a finding. Filling the hole is a fabrication.
  • Separate the builder from the build. Attribution dates and clusters work and helps you locate reasoning. It does not belong in a client-facing artifact, and never editorialize about the person.
Then split the plan

For each workstream, state what remains and in what order — separating work Zipcio can start immediately, work blocked on a client decision (name the decision and its owner), and work blocked on a third party (including anything unschedulable because the vendor has published no timeline). Where dates are asked for, resist blending controllable and uncontrollable work into one number: a single date that depends on a vendor's unpublished schedule makes Zipcio accountable for that vendor. Commit only to what we own.

Job 2 — before every client meeting, without exception

Prepare for a meeting

Meetings with Matt are scarce and should be spent on judgment, not briefing. If a system holds the answer, retrieve it yourself. Walk in able to answer these seven questions, and the meeting becomes a decision rather than a status recap.

Where each answer lives
  • Why this meeting exists — the calendar invite, the last meeting's summary, the recurring cadence in the engagement record.
  • What happened last time — meeting summaries first; the full transcript only if the summary shows a decision.
  • What was promised — the contracted scope in Drive, plus dated commitments in the engagement record and ClickUp comments.
  • What needs a decision — open ClickUp tasks with no owner or no date, and anything flagged as blocked.
  • What to inspect first — the client portal, through the client access layer, for the specific thing under discussion.

Preparation failures worth naming
  • Reading only the most recent call. The commitment that matters was probably made at kickoff.
  • Trusting a summary as the record. Summaries are for triage — they tell you which transcript to open.
  • Skipping the scope document because it feels like paperwork. It's the single most important statement of what the build was supposed to be.
  • Arriving with questions instead of a position. Questions are fine; a position plus questions is the standard.
  • Not inspecting the actual system before discussing its state. Ten minutes in the portal beats an hour of speculation.
Job 3 — same day, every time

After the meeting

Two halves, and they are not the same work. First you log it — the commitments made in that room are now real, and they exist nowhere but your memory until the record catches up. Then you calibrate it — which is how you learn to read a client situation the way the leadership layer does. Do the logging the same day. Do the calibration before you hear anyone else's read.

Half one — log it, same day

Run Engagement Sync. It pulls the meeting record alongside the project space and recent mail and chat, then walks you through three confirmation moments — existing task updates, then new tasks and subtask promotions, then attachments. You approve each set. It ends by drafting the client-facing follow-up for you to send.

What has to land in the record
  • Every commitment made in the room — what, who, by when. A commitment that lives only in your memory is one you will discover the hard way.
  • Decisions, with who made them. Not just the topic that was discussed.
  • Anything that changed — scope, dates, ownership, or the client's position on something they'd already agreed.
  • Blockers, named, with whose blocker it is: ours, theirs, or a third party's.
  • Your time, against the right list — the retainer or project list for meeting time, the engagement task list for task time. Check which exists rather than assuming.
  • The follow-up itself, sent by you after you've read it. No skill sends client communication.
How to log it well
  • Comment before you change status. Comments preserve why; status changes close doors. The project record is a roadmap, not a changelog.
  • Update before you add. Check whether a task already covers the topic — a second task on the same thread splits the history.
  • Never infer a due date. If it wasn't said, ask. An invented date becomes a commitment nobody made.
  • Log while it's fresh. Tomorrow you'll remember the decisions and lose the reasoning, and the reasoning is the half that matters later.
  • Read what Sync proposes. Approving a set blind defeats the point of there being three gates.
Half two — calibrate it

This is the primary mechanism for transferring judgment without hours of formal training. Answer all eleven before you hear Matt's read. Then compare. Where you agree, no discussion is needed — that's already calibrated. Where you differ, that difference is the entire agenda, and it's worth more than any walkthrough either of you could plan.

Job 4 — any new scope, no exceptions

Scope new work through the Cartographer

This is the one rule on this page with no judgment call attached. New scope goes through the Cartographer. Not a conversation, not a hunch formed on a call, not a proposal written from what the client said they wanted — a run of the skill, producing an audit and a structured recommendation.

Why it is the rule and not a preference

The Cartographer is what determines the structure of the engagement. Its scope ledger carries one row per scope unit, each with a horizon assignment and a project-or-retainer call, and that ledger is the bridge between what the audit found and the commercial motion — it is what the pricing engine consumes. Scope that never went through it is scope with no traceable derivation, which means that when the client challenges it in month three, nobody can reconstruct how we arrived at it. That is the failure this rule exists to prevent.

Run it when
  • Before a kickoff, or late in the sales process once portal access has been granted.
  • New scope appears on an account you already own — an expansion, a new workstream, a renewal that adds work.
  • Anyone asks what an engagement should cost or how long it should take. That answer comes out of a ledger, not out of your head.
How to invoke it

Slash command only — /cartographer. It will not trigger from conversation, deliberately. Expect ten to thirty minutes depending on portal complexity.

Do not run it when
  • The work is already sold and in delivery. That is the Strategist's lane, and a mid-engagement audit dressed as a scoping run wastes everyone's time.
  • You are reconstructing an inherited build. That is Job 1 and the Archaeologist — different question, different evidence.
  • You want a quick portal lookup. That is the Runner.
The accounts you are inheriting

Most of them predate the Cartographer — scoped before it existed, so there is no audit and no ledger behind them. Do not retro-fit a run onto delivery already in flight. Reconstruct instead, and reserve the Cartographer for the moment new scope actually appears.

What it needs from you before it starts
  • The client name, confirmed rather than assumed.
  • A read-scoped Private App token for their portal — CRM objects, properties, workflows, lists, forms, campaigns, sequences, pipelines, owners, reporting. Never stored beyond the session.
  • Which Hubs and which tier. Ask the client directly. This gates feature-adoption scoring and dimension selection, and it is not inferable from the portal.
  • Connectors verified. The client HubSpot access layer, the meeting record and Notion are blocking — missing any one and the run stops rather than producing a partial audit. Mail, Drive and deck generation are non-blocking, with the limitation named.

The standing rule on client-facing work

Everything client-facing traces back to the recommendation the close produced. For newly scoped work that is the Engagement Recommendations document — the audit's closing argument is what the engagement was sold on, and every deliverable, timeline and build decision after it should be recognisably downstream of it. For work already in delivery it is the session close package: the recap, the architecture brief, and the narrative agreed at the end of the session. Either way you are not inventing the client's picture of the work in a meeting or in an email. You are executing against a recommendation that already exists, and when it needs to change, the recommendation gets updated first — not quietly diverged from.

Two ways a run goes wrong
  • Running the wrong analytical shape for the portal in front of you. A twelve-year-old asset-dense portal and an eight-month-old sparse one demand different audits. Force one shape onto the other and you get either thin findings or manufactured ones.
  • Presenting a lower grade of evidence in the register of a higher one. Inference written as measurement is the fastest way to lose the client's trust in Zipcio — and yours in the skill. Watch for it in the draft and downgrade the language.
One more thing worth knowing

A portal that has been recently migrated can show thousands of deals with imported close dates and backfilled stages — enough records to run a regression and produce a confident, wrong answer. Migration artifacts are a harder failure than sparse data, because sparse data announces itself. If a portal looks suspiciously data-rich for its age, say so out loud before anyone scores it.

Exercise — do this in your first two weeks

Take an account you have inherited that has no Cartographer audit behind it. Working only from what you can retrieve, write down what its scope ledger would have said: the scope units, a horizon for each, and a project-or-retainer call. Then mark every row Attributed, Inferred or Unattributed. The proportion that comes out Inferred or Unattributed is a direct measure of how much of that engagement is running on assumption — and it is the most useful thing you can bring to a calibration session about that account.

Judgment

How we decide what to build — and who decides

This ladder and the three value categories below it are an approved Zipcio operating standard — use them as canonical, not as ramp shorthand. Zipcio is an AI-forward practice, which is exactly why AI is not the default answer. A client who buys an LLM workflow to paper over a broken process has bought a faster version of the wrong thing, and they will eventually notice. The discipline is to work down the ladder and stop at the first rung that actually solves the problem.

Productization

From one-off solve to product

Most of what we build starts as a specific answer to a specific client. Some of it should never be anything else. The skill is telling the difference early, because the cost of productizing the wrong thing is high and the cost of rebuilding the same thing four times is higher.

The path has four states. Know which one you are in and say so out loud.

01
Client solve

Built for one business, in their context, against their constraints. Most work stops here legitimately. A solve that only ever fits one client is not a failure; treating it as reusable is.

02
Candidate pattern

You have built something structurally similar more than once, or you can see the second client before finishing the first. The signal is structural repetition, not topical similarity — two clients wanting "lead routing" may share nothing underneath. Flag it. Do not start building for reuse yet.

03
Reusable pattern

The method is written down and someone other than the builder can run it. It has a name, a documented approach in Notion, and a stated boundary — what it covers and what it does not. Still delivered by a person exercising judgment.

04
Product

Repeatable enough to scope, price, sell and deliver against a known shape. Standardized intake, evidence trail, QA gates and handoff — with the architecture still reflecting the business in front of you. Productized does not mean generic. It means the repeatable parts are repeatable so the judgment parts get the attention.

What clears each gate
Awaiting Matt

The bar for 02 → 03 and 03 → 04, and who calls it. If the answer today is "you and I decide in conversation", that is what this should say — an honest informal process documented beats a formal one nobody follows.

Your role in this

Finding these is not a side project you get to after the delivery work. It is a primary expectation of the role, and it is the reason you are being pointed at a portfolio of accounts rather than one.

Bring candidates as you see them, with the second instance named. A pattern proposed from one engagement is a hunch.

Where AI creates value

Three categories. The strongest solutions combine them.

Approved operating standard
01 — Time
Reduce human effort or latency

The same output, materially faster or with materially less person-time. This is the easiest category to justify and the easiest to overclaim — if the manual version takes eleven minutes a week, the automation is a hobby, not a solution.

02 — Accuracy
Improve consistency, classification, analysis, or decision quality

The output is better, not just faster: consistent categorization, fewer missed signals, a defensible read where there was previously a guess. Often more valuable than time savings and consistently harder to sell, because the current error rate is usually invisible to the client.

03 — New capability
Make something possible that wasn't

Work that could not previously be done at all at a defensible cost — reading every call instead of sampling, reconstructing an undocumented build, monitoring a portfolio continuously. This is where the largest commercial arguments live.

A solution that saves time and raises accuracy and opens a capability the client didn't have is not three small wins — it's the shape of the work worth productizing.

Decision rights

Five levels — and you will sit at different ones simultaneously

Decision rights attach to a client and a workstream, not to how long you've been here. You may reach Level 4 on HubSpot architecture in your second week while sitting at Level 2 on Zipcio commercial framing for months. Name your level out loud when it's ambiguous; it costs one sentence and prevents the two expensive failures, which are asking permission for something you own and deciding something you don't.

Zipcio's output is the product. A correct build that's documented badly reads to the client as a build they can't trust — and the documentation is frequently the only artifact the executive sponsor ever sees. Assume the person reading your deliverable was not on the call.

Client-facing work traces back to the recommendation the close produced — the Engagement Recommendations for newly scoped work, the session close package for work in delivery. The quality bar
  • It sounds like Zipcio wrote it — not like a template was filled in. Direct, commercial, specific.
  • It explains why, not just what. The narrative thread gets established during the work, not reconstructed afterward.
  • Every claim has an anchor — a fill rate, an enabled state, a record count, a dated commitment.
  • Specificity over volume. Three findings that change a decision beat twelve that don't.
  • Visual quality is not decoration. Misaligned elements and text bleeding past a border tell the reader how carefully the build was done.
  • Zipcio-owed and client-owed are separated wherever both exist.
  • It's usable, not just correct. Someone acts on this tomorrow without asking you a clarifying question.

The exception is an explicit direction from the account lead to reference it.
  • Another client's data, examples, or context. Work outputs are siloed per client without exception.
  • Builder attribution in a client-facing artifact, unless the account lead explicitly asks for it.

Internal communication standard — bottom line up front

BLUF, for anything internal that carries a decision, risk, or ask

Applies to internal updates, delivery handoffs, escalation notes, leadership updates, and client-risk commentary. The first sentence answers one of: what is happening, what decision is needed, what risk exists, what you recommend, or what the reader should do. Put the conclusion first; keep the opening to one or two sentences; separate facts from assumptions from risks from recommendations; make ownership and timing explicit.

BLUF: [one-sentence conclusion — the actual point] Why it matters: [business or delivery consequence, 1–2 sentences] Key points: - [only what the reader needs to understand or act] - […] Ask / recommendation: [specific next step, owner, timing]

For a client-risk or escalation note, swap the middle for What changed → Impact → Options → Recommendation. Don't force BLUF where the goal is relationship repair, sensitive reassurance, or genuine brainstorming — stay concise, but soften the opening before moving to the operational point.

Where work lives

Four systems, four jobs — and one authority question

The routing question is almost always answerable: is this a method, a commitment, an artifact, or a record of what the client said? Method goes to Notion. Commitment goes to ClickUp. Artifact goes to Drive. What the client said stays in the meeting record and gets cited, not copied.

Notion — the method layer

The AI Knowledge Base & Control Center is the source of truth for reusable operating assets: prompts, methods and playbooks, meeting-workflow patterns, HubSpot scoping patterns, AI tool instructions, output templates, client context templates, governance rules, the commercial offer matrix, and reusable context packs. Skills pull canonical reference from it mid-session rather than relying on memory — which is why keeping it current is delivery work, not admin.

What belongs

Anything reusable across clients. One canonical record per asset. Deprecate aggressively when a better pattern replaces an older one.

What doesn't

Live project state (that's ClickUp), client deliverables (that's Drive), or a second copy of something that already has a canonical home.

How to tell whether it's authoritative — read the labels

Status: Draft is useful but not approved for broad reuse · Reviewed is checked but not canonical · Approved is reusable as a current operating asset · Deprecated is reference only.
Visibility: Internal-only may carry strategy, assumptions, pricing logic, or scoping risk · Client-safe may be reused externally when status and context fit.
Approved + Client-safe is the bar for direct client-facing use. Anything less, and you're the review step.

How to search it

Search by the operating noun, not the client — "escalation", "filter operator", "scoping pattern". Then check the status label and the last-edited date before you use what you found. When two sources conflict, prefer the newest authoritative operating material and surface the conflict rather than silently picking one.

ClickUp — the commitment layer

One space per client engagement. Inside it, the engagement's task structure plus time-tracking lists (retainer or project — check which exists, don't assume). Internal Zipcio time and internal meetings live in the internal space, never against a client space.

The governing principle

ClickUp is a roadmap, not a changelog. Comments preserve context; status updates close doors. Default to a comment. Update before you add — check whether a task already covers the topic before creating a new one, and promote a thread to a subtask of the existing parent rather than starting a sibling.

What must stay current

Anything a client could reasonably ask about: open commitments, owners, due dates, and blocked items. Recency is the source of truth — if the most recent activity contradicts the status, the status is wrong.

What you own here

Task structure, comments, dates, and status for accounts you own. Never infer a due date — ask when it's unclear. The default assignee is whoever is doing the work, not whoever created the task.

How it meets the skills

Engagement Sync reads the space alongside the meeting record and recent mail and chat, then proposes updates in three confirmation moments — existing task updates, then new tasks and subtask promotions, then attachments. You approve each. The ClickUp Runner does one atomic action with a one-line preview. If a request needs synthesis across sources, it belongs to Sync, not the Runner.

Google Drive — the artifact layer

One folder per client, named for the client. The contracted scope document, the persistent automation workbook, session narrative documents, layout specifications, and any client-facing file live there.

The convention you'll see

The automation workbook follows [Client] & Zipcio >> Documentation and Automation Log as a native Sheet. Session documents and specifications carry the client name and a date. The convention has drifted in practice — some older files use underscores or carry a tab-name suffix. Consequence: always search the whole Drive by filename pattern rather than scoping to the folder you expect, because a workbook that ended up in the wrong place is invisible to a folder-scoped search.

Link, don't duplicate

One authoritative copy. If you find a second, reconcile it and flag which one survives — a duplicated workbook means two people are about to log into different versions of the truth. Note that the available Drive tooling has no clean move primitive, so relocating a file means copying it and then cleaning up the original by hand; be explicit about that rather than implying a seamless move happened.

Source files stay put

Keep working files where they were produced and put the finished artifact in the client folder. Don't scatter drafts across the client folder — the client folder is what someone inheriting the account will read as the record.

The meeting record — the evidence layer

Recorded client conversations, transcripts, summaries, and participants. This is where you find what the client actually said, who was in the room, and when a decision was made. It is the highest-value source for reconstruction and the easiest one to over-pull.

How to use it well

Meeting list first — titles, dates, participants, whether a transcript exists. Summaries next; they're cheap and they tell you which calls carry decisions. Full transcripts only where the summary shows kickoff, requirements, decision language, a scope or timeline change, a new stakeholder, or a reversal.

The rule that protects everyone

Cite it, don't copy it. Transcripts and summaries stay in the authenticated system. Quote the sentence that carries the commitment, with its date and speaker, into the artifact that needs it. Never bulk-paste a transcript into a document, a chat, or anything hosted.

Also true

Time logged against a client meeting should be verified against actual attendance in the meeting record, not assumed from the calendar invite. If you weren't in it, it isn't your time.
